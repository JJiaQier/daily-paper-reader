# ABSTRACT 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently shown strong capabilities in reasoning, planning, and 

tool‑use, enabling new forms of autonomous agents. However, LLM‑based agents struggle with 

long‑horizon sequential decision tasks that require precise action optimization and environment 

interaction. Reinforcement Learning (RL), while effective for sequential control, often lacks the high‑level 

abstraction and task decomposition abilities needed for complex scenarios. This paper introduces an 

LLM‑Augmented Reinforcement Learning Agent that integrates LLM‑driven planning with RL‑based action 

optimization. The proposed architecture leverages the LLM to generate subgoals, structured plans, and 

contextual guidance, while the RL agent refines low‑level actions through interaction with the 

environment. Experiments on sequential decision tasks demonstrate improved sample efficiency, higher 

success rates, and more coherent action trajectories compared to RL‑only and LLM‑only baselines. This 

hybrid paradigm highlights a promising direction for building more capable autonomous systems.

# 1.  INTRODUCTION 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have recently demonstrated remarkable capabilities in 

reasoning, planning, and tool‑use, enabling a new class of autonomous systems known as agentic 

AI. These models can decompose tasks, generate structured plans, and interact with external 

tools, making them promising candidates for high‑level decision‑making. However, despite their 

strengths, LLM‑based agents struggle with long‑horizon sequential decision tasks, where precise 

action optimization, stable learning, and continuous environment feedback are essential. 

In parallel, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has established itself as a powerful framework for 

sequential decision‑making, achieving state‑of‑the‑art performance in robotics, control, games, and autonomous systems. RL agents excel at optimizing low‑level actions through trial‑and‑error 

interaction, but they often require extensive exploration, large training budgets, and carefully 

engineered reward functions. Moreover, RL lacks the ability to perform abstract reasoning, task 

decomposition, or symbolic planning, limiting its effectiveness in complex real‑world scenarios. 

This paper explores a hybrid paradigm that combines the strengths of both approaches. We 

propose an LLM‑Augmented Reinforcement Learning Agent, where the LLM acts as a high‑level 

planner generating subgoals, structured action sequences, and contextual guidance, while the RL 

agent refines low‑level actions through environment interaction. This integration aims to reduce 

exploration cost, improve sample efficiency, and enhance performance on complex sequential 

decision tasks. 

Our contributions are threefold: 

1.  We introduce a modular architecture that integrates LLM‑based planning with RL‑based 

action optimization. 

2.  We present a unified interaction loop enabling the LLM to guide exploration, reward 

shaping, and policy refinement. 

3.  We evaluate the hybrid agent on multiple sequential decision environments and 

demonstrate significant improvements over RL‑only and LLM‑only baselines. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on RL, LLM 

agents, and hybrid planning systems. Section 3 presents the proposed architecture and interaction 

mechanism. Section 4 describes the experimental setup. Section 5 reports quantitative and 

qualitative results. Section 6 discusses limitations and future directions. Section 7 concludes the 

paper .

# 2.  RELATED  WORK 

2.1.  Reinforcement Learning for Sequential Decision Tasks 

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has been widely studied as a framework for sequential 

decision‑making in complex environments. Foundational work such as Sutton and Barto’s 

textbook established the theoretical basis for value‑based and policy‑based methods. Modern 

algorithms including DQN, PPO, and SAC have achieved strong performance in robotics, 

control, and game‑playing. Research from DeepMind and FAIR has demonstrated RL’s ability to 

solve long‑horizon tasks, but these methods often require extensive exploration, large 

computational budgets, and carefully engineered reward functions. Recent Canadian 

contributions, notably from Mila and McGill, have emphasized reproducibility, sample 

efficiency, and offline RL benchmarks. 

2.2.  LLM Agents and Tool‑Use 

Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled a new class of autonomous agents capable of 

reasoning, planning, and interacting with external tools. Systems such as Voyager, Reflexion, and LLM‑AA demonstrate how LLMs can generate structured plans, refine actions through 

self‑reflection, and perform tool‑use in interactive environments. Microsoft Research, Stanford, 

and Princeton have shown that LLM agents can outperform traditional symbolic planners in 

open‑ended tasks. However, LLM‑based agents struggle with precise action optimization, 

long‑horizon consistency, and hallucination‑induced errors, limiting their effectiveness in 

sequential control tasks. 

2.3.  Hybrid Planning Systems (LLM + Symbolic + RL) 

Hybrid systems combining LLM reasoning with RL optimization have recently emerged as a 

promising direction. Approaches such as Plan‑and‑Solve (DeepMind), Language‑Conditioned 

RL, and hierarchical RL with LLM‑generated subgoals illustrate how high‑level planning can 

guide low‑level policy learning. Symbolic planners integrated with LLMs have also shown 

improvements in task decomposition and interpretability. Canadian research groups, including 

Mila and ULaval, have explored hybrid architectures for energy optimization, resource 

management, and intelligent control systems. 

2.4.  Gaps in Current Literature 

Despite rapid progress, several gaps remain. 

First, existing LLM agents lack robust mechanisms for long‑horizon sequential decision‑making, 

especially in environments requiring fine‑grained action optimization. Second, RL‑only systems 

struggle with abstract reasoning and task decomposition, leading to inefficient exploration. Third, 

current hybrid approaches often rely on ad‑hoc integration rather than unified architectures. 

Finally, few works provide systematic evaluations comparing RL‑only, LLM‑only, and hybrid 

RL+LLM agents on sequential decision tasks. 

This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a modular LLM‑Augmented RL agent with a 

unified interaction loop and comprehensive experimental evaluation. 

# 3.  PROPOSED FRAMEWORK: LLM -AUGMENTED RL AGENT 

3.1.  Overall Architecture 

The proposed agent integrates a Large Language Model (LLM) as a high‑level planner with a 

Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy responsible for low‑level action optimization. The 

architecture is modular and consists of four main components: 

(i) an LLM‑based planner generating subgoals and structured action sequences, 

(ii) an RL core optimizing environment‑specific actions, 

(iii) a memory module storing trajectories, plans, and feedback, 

(iv) an environment providing state transitions and rewards. 

This hybrid design is inspired by recent advances in LLM agents [6], tool‑use [8], and 

hierarchical RL [13], while addressing the limitations of RL‑only systems in long‑horizon tasks 

[2,4]. The architecture is designed to integrate high -level reasoning with low -level control in a coherent 

decision -making pipeline. The environment provides the agent with state information, which is 

interpreted by the LLM planner to produce subgoals and structured guidance. These high -level 

directives are then consumed by the RL policy, enabling more efficient and goal -directed action 

selection. A memory module records all transitions, plans, and actions, supporting both policy 

optimization and potential plan revision. This modular organization allows the LLM and RL 

components to complement each other, combining semantic reasoning with continuous control to 

address long -horizon sequential decision tasks.

3.2. LLM -Driven High -Level Planning 

Figure  2 – High Level Planning with  LLM LLMs have demonstrated strong capabilities in reasoning, task decomposition, and tool -use 

[6,7,9]. In our framework, the LLM acts as a high -level planner responsible for generating 

structured guidance that improves RL exploration and reduces sample complexity. 

▪ Prompting The LLM receives the current state sₜ and a textual description of the task. A 

carefully designed prompt instructs the model to produce: 

• subgoals gₜ 

• a sequence of high -level actions 

• contextual reasoning explaining the plan 

This approach follows the principles of Reflexion [7] and Voyager [6], where LLMs generate 

actionable plans through structured prompting. 

▪ Action Decomposition 

The LLM decomposes complex tasks into smaller actionable units. For example: 

“Navigate to the target, avoid obstacles, then collect the object.” 

This decomposition provides the RL agent with semantic guidance , similar to hierarchical RL 

with LLM -generated subgoals [13]. 

▪ Task Abstraction 

The LLM abstracts environment details into symbolic representations, enabling more coherent 

long -horizon planning. This aligns with language -conditioned RL [12] and LLM -guided 

symbolic planning [14]. 

3.3. RL Policy Optimization 

The RL core is responsible for optimizing low -level actions aₜ based on environment feedback. 

We use a standard RL formulation following Sutton & Barto [1]. 

Mathematical Formulation : The RL agent maximizes the expected cumulative reward 

The policy receives both the environment state sₜ and the LLM -generated subgoal gₜ :

This conditioning improves exploration efficiency, similar to hierarchical RL [13]. 

▪ LLM -Guided Reward Shaping 

The LLM provides semantic feedback that can be converted into auxiliary rewards. For example: 

• “You are close to the goal” → positive shaping 

• “You deviated from the plan” → negative shaping 

This technique is inspired by verbal reinforcement learning in Reflexion [7]. 

▪ Improved Exploration 

LLM -generated subgoals reduce the need for random exploration, addressing known RL 

limitations [2,4]. The agent explores purposefully , guided by high -level reasoning. 

3.4. Interaction Loop 

The interaction loop defines how the LLM planner and RL policy collaborate during execution. 

Sequential Interaction 1.  Environment provides state sₜ 

2.  LLM generates subgoal gₜ and plan πᴴ

3.  RL policy selects low -level action aₜ 

4.  Environment returns next state sₜ₊₁ and reward rₜ 

5.  Memory stores trajectory 

6.  RL updates policy 

7.  LLM revises plan if necessary 

This loop is consistent with hybrid planning systems such as Plan -and -Solve [11].

4.3 Metrics 

We use four quantitative metrics commonly used in hybrid RL -LLM research. 

→ Success Rate 

Percentage of episodes where the agent completes the task. 

→ Task Completion Efficiency 

Average number of steps required to reach the goal. Lower is better. 

→ Planning Accuracy 

Measures how often LLM -generated subgoals are correct or useful. Inspired by metrics used in 

SayCan [21,22] 

→ Cumulative Reward 

Total reward accumulated per episode. Standard RL metric.

# 6. D ISCUSSION 

6.1 System Strengths 

The proposed hybrid LLM -Augmented RL framework exhibits strong adaptability and reasoning 

capabilities across diverse environments. By combining semantic guidance from large language 

models with reinforcement learning optimization, the system achieves higher success rates, faster 

convergence, and improved planning efficiency. Moreover, the integration of natural -language 

feedback enhances interpretability, allowing transparent insight into the agent’s decision -making 

process. 

6. 2 Limitations 

Despite these advantages, the system remains computationally intensive due to the dual inference 

pipeline between the LLM and RL modules. Training stability can fluctuate depending on prompt 

quality and environmental complexity. Additionally, the reliance on pretrained LLMs introduces 

sensitivity to linguistic ambiguity and domain -specific terminology, which may hinder 

generalization in highly specialized tasks. 

6. 3 Risks 

Potential risks include hallucinations in language -based reasoning, where the LLM may generate 

inaccurate or inconsistent subgoals, and planning errors caused by misaligned reward signals. 

These issues can lead to inefficient exploration or unintended behaviors. Mitigation strategies 

such as confidence calibration, reward regularization, and human -in -the -loop validation are 

essential to ensure reliability and safety. 

6. 4 Perspectives 

Future research should aim to strengthen cross -modal consistency between textual reasoning and 

environmental feedback. Incorporating self -verification mechanisms, adaptive prompt tuning, and 

lightweight memory architectures could further improve robustness. Extending the framework to 

multi -agent collaboration and real -world embodied tasks represents a promising direction for 

scalable and trustworthy agentic AI systems. 7.  CONCLUSION 

The hybrid LLM -Augmented RL approach presented in this work demonstrates that combining 

language -based reasoning with reinforcement learning can substantially improve 

decision -making, efficiency, and overall task success. By leveraging structured subgoals, 

semantic reward shaping, and iterative feedback, the system achieves more stable learning and 

clearer planning behavior than traditional RL baselines. 

This hybrid paradigm is important because it bridges symbolic reasoning and continuous control, 

enabling agents to operate with both high -level understanding and low -level adaptability. Such a 

capability opens the door to richer applications in autonomous systems, interactive assistants, 

robotics, and complex resource -management tasks. 

Future applications may include multi -agent coordination, real -world embodied tasks, and 

adaptive planning in dynamic environments. Extensions could explore self -verification 

mechanisms, improved prompt tuning, and tighter integration between textual reasoning and 

environmental feedback to further enhance robustness and scalability.
